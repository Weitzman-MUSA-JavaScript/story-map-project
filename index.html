History of the The Singaporean Hawker Center

From unlicensed street vendors to a UNESCO Intangible Heritage Culture, scroll down to learn more about the history of Singapore's Hawker Centers.

The Beginnings: Street Vendors

While Hawker Centers today are known for world-renound food quality at relatively affordable prices, these open-air, market-style buildings were created as a response to a growing problem of unlicensed street vendoring.

Maxwell Road Food Center is known as one of the oldest Hawker Centers. It began as a wet market in 1929 and formally introduced the concept of having cooked food stalls and eating tables under one roof in 1935. While this initially faced challenges due to the lack of enforcement for street vendors gathering directly outside the Hawker Center, it laid the foundation for what we understand as the Hawker Center Today.

Source: National Heritage Board

Mid-1960s to Late-1970s

Following Singapore's independence from Malaysia in 1965, the newly established government set out to expand the hawker center program. Through an island-wide registration of street hawkers, the government began clamping down on illegal hawkering to ensure the hawkers were properly regulated and located within the newly established hawker centers. In the short span of 5 years (1974-1979), around 54 hawker centers were constructed all across the island to ensure good access to affordable food options for all residential estates.

Source: National Heritage Board

1980s to Present

The growth of Hawker Centers continued through the 1980s to the present. With the growing need to densify urban areas due to land scarcity, newer hawker centers were built with improved designs, and older hawker centers were renovated. Hawker Centers were also recognized as a UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2020, marking another step forward in preserving heritage and ensuring that the culinary culture continues into the future generations.

Source: National Heritage Board

The Future

As the country continues to grow, so does its need for affordable food options in newer suburban areas. Within the next 3 years, 6 more Hawker Centers will be completed, thus bringing greater accessibility to residents and workers in the surrounding areas.

Despite the growth in infrastructure, there have been concerns that the lack of talent renewal in the hawker space would result in the loss of Hawker culture on a whole. While many current Hawkers do not want their children to follow in their footsteps due to the intense labour and tough margins, some have still chosen to join the trade in an effort to keep the culture alive.

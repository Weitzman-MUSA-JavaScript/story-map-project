The Impact of SEPTA's Summer 2025 Service Cuts

As SEPTA, the public transit operator for the Philadelphia region, emerged from the pandemic, it faced structural funding issues as federal emergency funds ran out and subsidies from Pennsylvania government expired.

By the summer of 2025, while the PA legislature was deadlocked over the budget, SEPTA was forced to enact massive service cuts due to lack of resources.

This interactive storymap explores the impact of these service cuts on the SEPTA network.

Eliminated or Shortened Routes

Starting on August 24th, 32 bus routes, or 19% of all SEPTA routes, were completely eliminated from the network (routes shown in dark red). A further 16 routes—or 10% of routes—were shortened (shown in yelow).

The timing of the cuts also impacted school trips shortly after the term started, including the elimination or shortening of several school-specific routes.

Routes Entirely Eliminated

Though SEPTA attempted to limit complete route eliminations, 32 routes ceased service starting on August 24th. These routes included those which served dense areas like Route 12 and 47M, as well as suburban routes. Northeast Philadelphia saw an especially high number of bus routes eliminated from service.

Shortened Routes (before cuts)

Sixteen routes reduced the areas that they served. This map shows the reduced routes as they existed before the cuts; scrolling to the next map shows the extent of the route reductions .

Shortened Routes (after cuts)

Some routes which previously served Center City now stopped short (like Route 5). Others with long extensions into suburban areas drastically reduced their service areas.

The Remaining Network

The remaining routes suffered service decreases even if they kept the same routes. Decreased frequencies (with 10-minute frequencies turning into 15 minutes and 20-minute frequencies turning into 30 minutes) for remaining routes contributed to an overall 20% service reduction.

Possible Future: Regional Rail Cuts

Two weeks later, in early September, regular service was restored—but at a steep cost. Because the state legislature could not agree on funding which would have addressed the structural problem, SEPTA was forced to raid its capital funding, reducing the resources available to renovate stations, make safety improvements, and purchase new trains.

Later in 2025, Regional Rail riders experienced severe disruptions as a series of 50-year old trains caught on fire, demonstrating the risks of reducing resources for equipment and other capital needs.

Though the stopgap funding temporarily removed the specter of five Regional Rail lines being eliminated (shown in dark red), without a permanent funding solution it is inevitable that cuts like these will be threatened again in the future.
